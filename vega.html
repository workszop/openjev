A model that only decides

Vega never writes text. A frozen Qwen3.5 0.8B reads your state and a typed question once, then a small physics engine settles a particle into one of the options you listed. Every answer is a calibrated probability with a prediction set and an abstain flag.

How Vega reaches an answer

The prompt is fixed: Observation: then your state, Measurement (type): then the instructions, and one * option line per option. Short states also get the question up front, so the model reads with it in mind.

A frozen Qwen3.5 0.8B runs once over that prompt and stops at layer 19; its language-model head never runs. The page averages hidden states over the state span, the question span and each option span, and keeps the last token.

A 14M-parameter engine places a particle in a 64-dimensional decision space, puts one Gaussian well under each option and integrates 12 damped steps. The share of the particle in each well is the answer, softened by a temperature fitted to how settled it is (thermal calibration).

Two adapters, public_intents and typed_decisions, each have a gate. When a gate opens past 0.5, that adapter and its own calibration take over; the card shows which one answered.

Why Vega and Qwen3 can disagree

Both see the same state, the same instructions and the same option texts, word for word (for a boolean: "no - …" and "yes - …"; for a score: "level 0 - …" and up). They read them differently.

Qwen3 0.6B gets a chat prompt that lists the options as letters A, B, C and asks for one letter. Its numbers are the softmax of its next-token logits over those letters: a real readout, but not calibrated, and sensitive to option order and wording.

Vega never answers in words. It reads hidden states of a frozen Qwen3.5 0.8B and lets a trained engine settle on an option, then applies a temperature fitted on held-out data, so its confidence is meant to match how often it is right. It also tells you when to abstain.

So agreement on the top answer is the useful signal; the probabilities themselves are on different scales. A score question is compared on its most likely level.

Reading the flags
  • Confidence is the top probability after calibration.
  • 90% set is a split-conformal prediction set: options that cannot be ruled out at the 10% error level. One label means the model is sure enough; several mean it is torn between them.
  • Abstain fires when confidence falls below the threshold calibrated for a 10% risk. Treat the answer as "ask a human".
  • Unbound means the particle settled far from every well: the input looks out of distribution.
  • Steps and energy come straight from the engine; fewer steps means it settled early.
